Welding Types

TIG Welding

TIG welding (Tungsten Inert Gas) is a precise welding process that uses a non-consumable tungsten electrode to create an arc that melts metal. A shielding gas, usually argon, protects the weld from contamination.

How It Works

  • The tungsten electrode creates a high-temperature arc
  • The arc melts the base metal
  • A filler rod may be added by hand
  • Shielding gas protects the weld from air

Advantages

  • Produces clean and strong welds
  • Great for thin materials
  • Works well on aluminum and stainless steel
  • Offers high precision and control

Disadvantages

  • Slower than other welding methods
  • Requires more skill to learn
  • Equipment can be more expensive

MIG Welding

MIG welding (Metal Inert Gas) uses a continuously fed wire electrode and shielding gas to join metal quickly and efficiently.

How It Works

  • A welding gun feeds wire automatically
  • An electric arc melts the wire and base metal
  • The wire acts as filler material
  • Shielding gas protects the weld from contamination

Advantages

  • Easy to learn and beginner-friendly
  • Fast welding speed
  • Produces clean welds with little cleanup
  • Works well on steel and aluminum

Disadvantages

  • Less precise than TIG welding
  • Not ideal for outdoor use due to wind
  • Equipment can be bulky

Stick Welding

Stick welding (Shielded Metal Arc Welding) uses a consumable electrode coated in flux to create strong welds. The flux protects the weld from contamination.

How It Works

  • An electric current creates an arc between the rod and the metal
  • The rod melts and becomes the filler material
  • The flux coating creates protective gas and slag
  • Slag must be chipped off after welding

Advantages

  • Simple and low-cost equipment
  • Works well outdoors
  • Good for thick and rusty metals
  • Portable and versatile

Disadvantages

  • Requires more skill
  • Produces smoke and spatter
  • Leaves slag that needs cleanup
  • Slower than MIG welding

Flux-Cored Welding

Flux-cored arc welding (FCAW) uses a hollow wire filled with flux to join metal. The flux creates a protective shield during welding.

How It Works

  • A wire electrode is continuously fed through a welding gun
  • An arc melts the wire and base metal
  • The flux produces gas and slag for protection
  • Slag must be removed after welding

Advantages

  • Works well outdoors
  • No shielding gas needed (in some types)
  • Good for thick materials
  • Fast process

Disadvantages

  • Produces more fumes
  • Requires cleanup
  • Not as clean-looking as TIG or MIG
